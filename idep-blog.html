IDEP

We started by asking how to evaluate creativity in model capabilities. Conversations with many researchers revealed a different problem. Benchmarks already exist, but industry rarely values or adopts them. We then asked how creativity could improve real task completion. Research on agents using objects in simulated worlds to solve unexpected physical tasks gave us a direction. We now see 3D simulation as a way to make creative reasoning useful in messy environments. Our next step is to choose one focused vertical, potentially the home or another domain, and one problem to solve. This is the question we most want to discuss.

01 · Initial direction

Michelins for AI Benchmark Research

TL;DR

  • Why: We wanted to understand why strong AI evaluation research often fails to reach industry.
  • Key points: The main gaps are visibility, adoption, and interpretation. Interactive tests and expert review can make capability evidence easier to understand and trust.
  • Finding: Useful benchmarks should evaluate the full system around task execution, not only the foundation model.

1. The Model

The first Michelin Guide was published in France in 1900. It listed maps, petrol stations, and other resources intended to encourage motorists to travel farther, wear out their tires, and purchase more.

Over time, the guide developed into a credible restaurant rating system. Anonymous inspectors paid for their own meals and produced independent assessments. What began as marketing became trusted evaluation infrastructure.

2. The Gap

AI research and industry lack a comparable system for discovering, interpreting, and validating evaluation methods. New benchmark and data-pipeline research appears faster than most people can meaningfully assess it.

Visibility
Independent research is difficult to discover unless it is continuously maintained, promoted, or associated with a well-known lab.
Adoption
Industry teams rarely have enough time to follow every paper or determine which methods matter in their own application context.
Interpretation
Benchmark scores describe performance numerically, but many people cannot see what a model can actually do through those numbers alone.

The result is a visibility gap: research with strong institutional reach receives attention, while other useful methods remain difficult to find and evaluate.

3. The Ecosystem

What if evaluating a model were as accessible as playing a game on Steam? Users could interact with capabilities directly, compare how models behave, and discuss the differences through visible evidence.

Expert “inspectors” could then test whether a capability creates value inside a real application. Their extended reviews would document the model, evaluation method, use context, and observed outcome.

This evidence could help industry select models, help users understand capabilities, and give researchers feedback about what deserves further development. Together, these exchanges could form a healthier loop between research, users, and industry.

Current takeaway. Most companies evaluate the foundation model. What matters increasingly is the architecture and systems surrounding task execution. We may need benchmarks that evaluate these systems as a whole.

02 · Case study

Arena.ai Competitive Analysis

TL;DR

  • Why: We studied Arena to understand how an evaluation method earned industry trust and adoption.
  • Key points: Arena combined direct human comparisons with open access, frequent leaderboard updates, research visibility, and reusable datasets. Bradley Terry fits fixed model strengths better than sequential Elo.
  • Finding: Adopted benchmarks align with human preferences, separate models with confidence, and use fresh prompts.

1. Origin

Arena began as a response to a credibility problem. The team trained Vicuna-13B by fine-tuning LLaMA on 70,000 user-shared conversations collected through ShareGPT and built FastChat as an open-source toolkit for training and evaluating chat models.

Early evaluation relied on GPT-4 as a judge, but a single automated score was not persuasive enough to establish that Vicuna performed better. Arena introduced direct, pairwise model comparisons based on human votes.

Why It Gained Traction

  • Competition among proprietary and open-source models was intense, and many early voters were developers.
  • Users could access GPT-4, Claude, and PaLM 2 for free inside the arena.
  • The team published an updated leaderboard every two weeks.
  • The MT-Bench paper gave the evaluation method research visibility.
  • Open datasets made the system useful beyond the product itself.

2. Ranking System

Elo

Arena initially used Elo because it was familiar and had already appeared in Anthropic’s LLM research.

EA = 1 / (1 + 10((RB − RA) / 400))
RA′ = RA + K · (SA − EA)

Elo updates ratings one comparison at a time, so results depend on vote order and the selected K value. That behavior makes sense for chess players whose skill changes, but models remain fixed during evaluation.

Bradley Terry

P(A beats B) = 1 / (1 + e(ξB − ξA))
  • Each model receives one fitted strength value, ξ.
  • All votes are fitted together through logistic regression.
  • Each battle becomes one row, with +1 for model A and −1 for model B.
  • A tie counts as half a win and half a loss.
  • The fitted strength is rescaled to resemble Elo, making a 100-point difference roughly equivalent to a 64% win probability.

Potential Upgrades

Davidson / Rao Kupper
Model ties directly instead of converting them into partial wins and losses.
Crowd-BT
Weight voters according to their estimated reliability.
Prompt-to-Leaderboard
Estimate model rankings conditionally for different kinds of prompts.
Mixture Models
Represent different user preferences instead of forcing every vote into one shared ranking.

3. Arena-Hard

A stronger benchmark depends on three qualities:

Human Agreement
Scores should correlate with human preferences in real-world use cases.
Separability
Scores should include confidence intervals and distinguish models with high confidence.
Freshness
Prompts should be new and unseen to reduce test leakage.

Read the Arena-Hard blog or visit the GitHub repository.

03 · Interview synthesis

From AI Creativity to 3D Agents

TL;DR

  • Why: We interviewed creativity researchers to clarify what should be measured and what evidence matters.
  • Key points: Creativity evaluation must separate outcome, behavior, and process. A strategy should work within its environment, differ in its logic, and create value over time.
  • Finding: 3D simulation can reveal how a strategy emerges and whether it transfers across changing environments.

1. Define

The interviews revealed that AI creativity research often examines different objects.

Cheng Qian
In EscapeBench and CreativityBench, creativity appears as unconventional but effective tool use within a constrained action space.
Antonios Liapis
Creativity cannot be reduced to a final artifact. It emerges through interaction, selection, and shifts in direction.
Vijeta Deshpande
A shared capability appears across creativity tasks, but it cannot explain why an output matters in a specific context.

These different definitions also lead researchers to evaluate creativity at different levels.

Outcome
Task completion, novelty, and usefulness. Easy to compare; weak at explaining how a solution emerged.
Behavior
Tools used and strategy selected. Reveals meaningful difference, but may mistake randomness for exploration.
Process
Departure from the common path, response to failure, and triggers for strategy shifts. Shows how ideas evolve and influence later decisions.

These levels answer three different questions: what result was produced, what method was used, and how that method was discovered.

2. Reframe

Moving away from a universal creativity score does not mean discarding the earlier research. The interviews helped us preserve three forms of evidence that can travel across domains:

Outcome
Whether the agent solved the problem and whether the result was effective and reproducible.
Process
How the agent explored, failed, gathered information, and changed direction, including whether an early attempt became valuable later.
Strategy
Which approach the agent ultimately used and whether it truly differed from the common solution.

Using this evidence, we can replace the broad judgment “Is this behavior creative?” with three more specific judgments: Does it work within the environmental constraints? Does its strategic logic differ? Does it create value over time?

3. Ground

Qiancheng’s research provides a key bridge. In EscapeBench, an agent completes escape-room-like tasks using available objects and actions. Success remains objectively verifiable, but the correct intermediate steps are not prescribed.

CreativityBench extends this idea to unconventional object use. Together, the benchmarks combine a clear goal with an open solution space where behavior leaves verifiable consequences.

In 3D simulation, agents act through spatial relationships and environmental feedback. Researchers can replay complete trajectories and systematically vary the world.

The shift. Qiancheng’s work asks whether an agent can find an unconventional but effective solution; 3D simulation lets us ask how that solution emerged and whether it transfers.

04 · Current direction

Simulation in 3D Worlds

TL;DR

  • Why: We needed to identify where 3D simulation creates value beyond interviews, spreadsheets, and conventional simulation.
  • Key points: Behavioral simulation predicts coordination. Physical simulation tests action with spatial consequences. The two directions are high stakes collective orchestration and embodied task execution.
  • Finding: We should choose one focused vertical where 3D is essential, potentially a task in the home.

1. Distinguish

Simulating agent-to-agent communication in a three-dimensional setting is different from placing agents inside a world governed by physical properties.

Behavioral Simulation
Models how people or agents communicate, coordinate, and make decisions inside a spatial context.
Physical Simulation
Models movement, object interaction, spatial constraints, and embodied task execution, as required in robotics.

The first predicts behavior. The second tests whether an agent can act successfully in a world with physical consequences. Our next decision is which of these systems creates a meaningful and testable form of enterprise value.

The Simile Reference

Simile applies behavioral prediction to retail and merchandising contexts, including work associated with CVS. Its direction moved away from a persistent 3D environment and toward collecting richer human-behavior data through interviews.

When does representing behavior in 3D add value that cannot be produced through interviews, spreadsheets, or conventional simulation?

2. Orchestrate

One direction is large-scale orchestration: using simulation to compare plans before many people must execute them under time pressure.

The useful cases share three properties: many people, a short execution window, and a high cost of failure.

Retail
Coordinate merchandising, staffing, customer flow, and operational changes across a store.
Plant Turnaround
Plan maintenance logistics when a refinery or industrial plant must stop, service equipment, and restart quickly.
Hospital Move Day
Coordinate staff, patients, equipment, routes, and dependencies during a facility transition.
Architecture Planning
Test how layouts influence movement, collaboration, congestion, and use before construction or reconfiguration.

3. Embody

A second direction is to simulate physical environments and evaluate how agents manipulate objects, navigate constraints, and discover effective strategies.

Generated Homes
Create diverse 3D domestic environments for training and evaluating robots on household tasks.
Drone Airspace
Simulate traffic, separation, and detect-and-avoid behavior for operations beyond the pilot’s visual line of sight.

The FAA has proposed Part 108 as a framework for routine beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations. This creates a timely setting for testing how autonomous systems detect aircraft, respond to conflict, and coordinate safely in shared airspace.

Read the FAA proposed BVLOS rule.

Open decision. Simulate collective behavior, or test embodied task execution?
